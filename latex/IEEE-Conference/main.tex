\begin{abstract}
Road-damage detectors must distinguish defects from other road-surface objects while maintaining useful performance outside their training domain. We compared five compact YOLO detectors on the six-class Asian subset of N-RDD2024, comprising four damage classes and two additional surface-object classes. Mean average precision (mAP) at an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 is the primary metric. We tested the six-class models on an Indonesian road image set without fine-tuning, with the main transfer comparison restricted to the same four damage classes in both domains. On the 995-image source test set, our reimplementation of YOLO-RD achieved the highest six-class mAP at IoU 0.5, 0.661, followed by YOLOv12n at 0.654. On the 307-image target set, YOLOv12n obtained the highest four-damage-class mAP at IoU 0.5, 0.413, versus 0.626 on the same classes of the source test set, the smallest relative decrease among the five models (34.1\%). \ifdistractoranalysis Four-class versus six-class training produced small, mixed changes in damage-class mAP at IoU 0.5 and did not establish a benefit from explicitly labeling the two additional classes.\fi{} These single-seed results show that source-domain ranking and zero-shot ranking need not coincide.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Road-damage detectors must separate structural damage from visually similar non-damage surface objects under variation in pavement appearance, viewpoints, and camera conditions. Multi-country road-damage datasets support evaluation beyond the training distribution \cite{arya2021deep,Arya2024846}. Prior work identifies manhole covers and road patches as potential visual distractors in pothole detection \cite{matouq2024ai,bucko2022computer,Shi_2026}, motivating a comparison of training with and without these labels for core damage detection in compact YOLO models.

Recent road-damage studies propose custom YOLO variants for lower computation or richer feature extraction and compare them with selected baselines \cite{Li2024,Zeng2024,Wang2025}. Comparative studies have also evaluated successive YOLO architectures under shared training configurations for road-damage detection \cite{tilmisani2025road}. These studies motivate a joint comparison of task-specific variants and newer native models, including YOLOv12n and YOLO26n, under a common experimental protocol. Because the studies use different datasets and protocols, their scores are not directly comparable across papers. Moreover, a detector selected solely by its source-domain score may perform differently on unseen road images. We therefore ask how five compact detectors compare on a common six-class source test set and how much performance they retain on an unseen target dataset without fine-tuning.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Datasets and Cross-Domain Evaluation}
RDD2022 assembled multi-national road images around four core crack and pothole categories \cite{Arya2024846}, while earlier cross-country studies motivated testing detectors beyond their training distribution \cite{arya2021deep}. However, prior image-based pothole detectors often suffer from false alarms caused by visually similar non-damage surface features \cite{bucko2022computer}. The SVRDD dataset added manhole covers as a separate class after they were confused with potholes \cite{Shi_2026}. Bu{\v{c}}ko et al. likewise used separate labels for potholes and manhole covers because their visual similarity can cause misclassification, while Matouq et al. advocated joint training across potholes, manholes, and road patches to reduce false detections \cite{bucko2022computer,matouq2024ai}. N-RDD2024 expanded road defect annotations to ten categories that include manholes and patched roads \cite{kaya2024nrdd2024}.

\subsection{Compact Detection Architectures}
YOLO performs object detection in a single-stage framework \cite{redmon2016you}, and many road-damage studies modify its structure for either mobile efficiency or better sensitivity to small defects. Most custom variants add lightweight convolutions, attention modules, or feature-fusion blocks to reduce computation or suppress background noise \cite{Wu2023,Li2024,Shi_2026}. Comparative evaluations of pothole detectors further highlight the need to consider both detection accuracy and inference speed when selecting models for road monitoring \cite{rianto2025pothole}. To compare these design choices, we reimplement two recent road-damage models. YOLOv8-PD \cite{Zeng2024} focuses on parameter efficiency by combining Ghost-based blocks \cite{han2020ghostnet}, attention modules, and a shared-convolution head. Conversely, YOLO-RD \cite{Wang2025} prioritizes richer feature extraction for small defects and complex backgrounds using star-operation blocks, auxiliary neck fusion, and a wavelet-convolution head \cite{finder2024wtconv}. This comparison examines how the two task-specific designs perform against native Ultralytics models, namely YOLOv8n, YOLOv12n \cite{tian2025yolov12_primary}, and YOLO26n \cite{sapkota2025yolo26_primary}, under a broadly shared training policy.

\subsection{Source and Target Data}
To test the visual-distractor hypothesis discussed in Section II-A, we extracted the Asian subset (China, Japan, and India) of N-RDD2024 \cite{kaya2024nrdd2024} and filtered its ten original categories into six labels. These comprise four structural damage classes, namely longitudinal crack (D00), transverse crack (D10), alligator crack (D20), and pothole (D40), alongside two pothole-resembling surface classes, namely manhole (D70) and patchy road (D80). The remaining categories were outside the scope of this comparison. The train, validation, and test splits were formed by multilabel stratified sampling at a 70:20:10 ratio, which preserves class proportions across splits, and contained 6,777, 1,898, and 995 images, respectively, with 17,092, 4,773, and 2,425 label instances in the split inventory. \ifdistractoranalysis The corresponding four-class label inventories contained 14,351, 3,996, and 2,035 instances; images were retained when the two additional labels were removed.\fi{} Patchy road had 471 training instances, compared with 4,555 longitudinal-crack instances, a 9.7-fold count difference; manhole had 2,270 training instances. \ifsourceexamples Fig.~\ref{fig:source-classes} illustrates the six source labels.

The target set, RDD-Indo, was collected using a road-facing Android smartphone at 1080p resolution in both traffic directions, primarily along Jalan Raya Bojongsoang and on nearby streets in Bandung Regency, Indonesia. Videos were sampled at one frame per second. The selected images were annotated by one annotator in Roboflow and center-cropped to $640 \times 640$ pixels to match the model input size. The set contains 307 images and 502 annotations: 481 instances across the four damage classes and 21 patchy-road instances. No manholes were annotated, and 39 images contain background only. Worn, irregular repairs were retained as patchy-road annotations even when their boundaries were partly indistinct. Here, zero-shot evaluation means applying source-trained detectors to the Indonesian target set without fine-tuning; the evaluated damage categories are already known from source training.

\subsection{Compared Models and Training}



We evaluated YOLOv8n, YOLOv12n \cite{tian2025yolov12_primary}, YOLO26n \cite{sapkota2025yolo26_primary}, and compact Ultralytics reimplementations of YOLOv8-PD and YOLO-RD \cite{Zeng2024,Wang2025}. The reimplementations include selected design elements rather than exact reproductions of the authors' models. Both use nano depth and width scaling. In YOLO-RD, the auxiliary fusion is implemented with Upsample, Concat, and $1 \times 1$ convolution rather than the original weighted-sum formulation. Native models used pretrained checkpoints, while the custom reimplementations loaded only compatible YOLOv8n weights. All five six-class models were trained with 640-pixel inputs, batch size 16, and seed 42 for up to 300 epochs, with early stopping at patience 40. Training used Ultralytics' automatic optimizer selection with weight decay set to 0.0005. The best checkpoint was selected using validation-set Ultralytics fitness. Shared augmentations were Mosaic (1.0), MixUp (0.05), horizontal flip (0.5), translation (0.10), and scale (0.50); rotation was disabled. To account for unequal training-instance counts across classes, we applied effective-number-derived per-class weights ($\beta=0.999$) to the binary-cross-entropy classification component of the detection loss \cite{cui2019class}.

Automatic mixed precision was enabled for the three native models and disabled for the two custom reimplementations. YOLO26n followed the shared experimental policy, which may differ from its architecture-specific recommended recipe. Source-domain training and throughput measurement used an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 SUPER 12 GB GPU. Throughput was measured at 640-pixel input with Ultralytics 8.4.108, PyTorch 2.5.1, and CUDA 12.1. Training time is wall-clock time until early stopping. The comparison evaluates these model implementations under the tested settings.

\subsection{Metrics and Comparison Rules}
We report mean average precision (mAP) at intersection-over-union (IoU) 0.5 and averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95, together with macro-F1. These are unweighted class averages of average precision (AP) and F1, respectively. Macro-F1 is computed from the per-class precision and recall reported by Ultralytics at the confidence threshold that maximizes the smoothed mean F1 on the evaluated split. \ifdistractoranalysis The training-label comparison uses only D00, D10, D20, and D40 under both training regimes.\fi{} The zero-shot comparison evaluates D00, D10, D20, and D40 in both domains, following the four damage categories used in RDD2022 \cite{Arya2024846}. The six-class model weights and all target images are retained. Results including patchy road are provided in the supplementary analysis. We define the relative decrease as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{rel},K} = 100\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{mAP}^{\mathrm{target}}_{K}}{\mathrm{mAP}^{\mathrm{source}}_{K}}\right)\%,
\label{eq:relative-drop}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the number of evaluated classes, not the number of training classes. We use $K=4$ in Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and $K=5$ in the supplementary analysis. Source and target AP evaluations used 640-pixel inputs, a minimum confidence of 0.001, and non-maximum suppression (NMS) IoU threshold 0.6 where applicable. Equation~\eqref{eq:relative-drop} summarizes a performance difference between these test sets, not the magnitude of domain shift or the causal effect of pavement texture, lighting, or location.

\ifdistractoranalysis As an additional diagnostic, we count pothole predictions with confidence $\geq 0.25$ that overlap at least one annotated manhole or patchy-road box at IoU $\geq 0.5$. Each prediction is counted once, even if it overlaps multiple distractor boxes; multiple predictions may overlap the same object. This is a prediction-box count, not an object-level false-positive rate.\fi




\section{Results and Discussion}
\subsection{Source-Domain Detection and Computational Trade-off}
% REVIEW-METHOD [resolved 2026-10-12]: FPS protocol verified from docs/RESULTS.md logs (RTX 4070 SUPER 12282MiB, Ultralytics 8.4.108, torch 2.5.1+cu121, Python 3.12.3); mAP GPU-independence re-verified on RTX 3050 (mAP50 0.6476 identical).
Table~\ref{tab:source} reports the six-class source test results. YOLO-RD obtained the highest mAP@0.5 (0.661) and macro-F1 (0.633). Its mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.371 was tied, to three decimal places, with YOLOv12n. YOLO26n had the lowest GFLOPs (5.3), while YOLOv8-PD used fewer parameters (2.47 M) than the YOLOv8n baseline and had the highest measured throughput. The compactness of our YOLOv8-PD reimplementation is consistent with the efficiency-oriented design of the original model \cite{Zeng2024}. YOLO-RD had the highest GFLOPs (12.8). These rankings are observations from one run per model, not statistically resolved differences or evidence of intrinsic superiority. Training time ranged from 4.4 to 11.7 h, with YOLO-RD completing the most epochs (278). The custom models also reused fewer pretrained weights. Their training costs reflect differences in implementation, precision settings, and stopping epochs, so these results do not isolate the effect of architecture.

Across the five six-class runs, pothole AP@0.5 ranged from 0.526 to 0.541, below the corresponding longitudinal- and alligator-crack values. Manhole AP@0.5 ranged from 0.820 to 0.845, whereas patchy-road AP@0.5 ranged from 0.512 to 0.587 with only 69 test instances.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Transfer and Domain Shift}
Table~\ref{tab:transfer} compares the same four damage classes in both domains using the six-class models. All models scored lower on the target set. YOLOv12n had the highest target mAP@0.5 (0.413), compared with 0.626 on the source test, and the smallest relative decrease among the five models (34.1\%). It therefore showed the strongest observed zero-shot transfer on this Indonesian target set. YOLO-RD led the four-damage-class source comparison (0.633) but reached 0.359 on the target, below YOLOv12n. This performance gap is consistent with domain shift between the source and Indonesian road images, but does not identify its causes. For YOLOv12n, target longitudinal-crack AP@0.5 (0.629) remained higher than transverse-crack AP (0.256) and pothole AP (0.260). \ifmaterialanalysis Selected target predictions are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:target-cases}.\fi

This could contribute to its transfer performance, but the present experiments do not isolate the effect of attention from other architectural and training differences.

\subsection{Limitations}
 The custom models are reimplementations with partial pretrained-weight transfer, limiting architecture-only comparisons. Target images cover a small area around Jalan Raya Bojongsoang and nearby streets, so geographic generalization is limited. Target labels were produced by one annotator, and inter-annotator agreement was not assessed. The visual examples do not measure error frequencies. The measured GPU throughput is hardware-specific and is neither an edge-device benchmark nor a universal real-time guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}
Our YOLO-RD reimplementation achieved the highest six-class source mAP@0.5 (0.661), while YOLOv12n achieved the highest four-damage-class zero-shot target mAP@0.5 (0.413) and the smallest relative decrease (34.1\%). The ranking changed on the local target set even when both domains were evaluated on the same four damage classes. \ifdistractoranalysis The paired training-label comparison did not establish a consistent benefit from adding manhole and patchy-road labels for core damage detection.\fi{}

The models showed different strengths: YOLO-RD led source accuracy, YOLOv12n led transfer to the Indonesian target set, and YOLOv8-PD had the highest measured throughput. Source accuracy alone was therefore insufficient to identify the best model for this target set. Repeated-seed training and evaluation across more road environments are needed to determine whether these rankings hold beyond the tested data and hardware.
